# Writing style — authentic student engineering voice

The report's originality mark and its credibility both depend on it reading like one specific
person wrote it after a real placement. The goal is good technical writing in an authentic
student voice, not decorated prose.

## Voice

- First person, past tense for events ("I traced the fault to..."), present tense for
  explanations of principles ("A subnet mask separates...").
- British/Nigerian English: organisation, programme, colour, centre, licence (noun), travelling.
- Plain and confident. A 200-level student writes with competence, not executive polish.
- Name real, specific things: the port number, the command, the screen, the symptom, the
  error message. Specifics are what make writing sound human.

## Paragraph shape for experience sections

Situation (what was wrong or needed) → what I did, step by step → the concept or tool
behind it → how I knew it worked → what I took from it.

Do not use this shape mechanically for every paragraph. Vary the order and length.

## Rhythm

- Mix short and long sentences. Some paragraphs may be only two or three sentences.
- Do not open consecutive paragraphs with the same word or structure.
- Use a short list or table only where the content genuinely is a list or a comparison
  (tools, steps, challenges). Everything else is prose.

## Words and habits to avoid

**Vocabulary ban (never use):**
delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, realm, landscape (as a metaphor), underscores (verb),
seamless, robust (unless describing a physical build), leverage, harness, foster,
multifaceted, "plays a crucial role", invaluable, incredible, transformative, unparalleled,
additionally (as a sentence opener), vibrant, nestled, breathtaking, groundbreaking (figurative),
renowned, showcasing, boasts, enduring, garnering, highlighting (as a closing -ing phrase),
fostering, cultivating, encompassing, reflecting broader (significance), marks/represents a shift.

**Structural bans:**
- Filler openers: "In today's fast-paced world", "It is worth noting that",
  "In conclusion, it can be seen that."
- Em dashes (—). Use commas, full stops or brackets instead.
- "Not only... but also" more than once in the document.
- Rule-of-three lists as a reflex. If three items happen naturally, fine; do not force it.
- Claims with no source: "studies show", invented percentages, invented downtime figures.
- Describing the placement as a "journey."
- Bold inside paragraph bodies (use only in tables and headings).

## Evidence discipline

- Every technical claim should trace to the student's evidence, a datasheet, a textbook
  principle, or ordinary engineering knowledge you are sure of.
- Where you drafted an activity, keep its detail at the level any business of that kind
  would plausibly have. No invented names or numbers.
- Explanations of theory must be correct. If you write Ohm's law, a subnet calculation,
  or a truth table, check the arithmetic.

## Linking to theory (the 5-mark section)

Write the principle, then show it at work with specific details.

Weak: "My knowledge of networking helped me configure the router."

Strong: "A /26 mask leaves 6 host bits, so each subnet holds 62 usable addresses.
That is why the 40 POS terminals in the shop fitted one subnet with room to spare, and
the IT team did not need to add a second switch."

## Final read-through

Read the document as the examiner would. Mark any sentence that could appear unchanged
in another student's report — and replace it with something only this student could have
written.
